Questions:

(We can ask any team member to explain any of the answers below
 in terms of your team's solution).

1) What was the impact of having noisy sensors in your ability to
   determine the state of the craft and ultimately control it
   properly?

   - Noisy sensors made it so that we couldn't just trust a single reading of a sensor.
   Instead, every frame, we sample each sensor 12000 times (NSAMPLES) and take the mean,
   which virtually eliminates random noise. We also use the variance of those samples
   to determine whether a sensor is broken.
   - The controls are noisy, but we noticed that they were also biased too. So we incorportated
   additive and multiplicative biases to remedy this. We had to measure all of this
   to be able to predict where the ship would be (on average).

2) Describe the technique(s) you used to deal with sensor noise

- Averaging: 12000 samples per sensor per frame, cached so every function that needs a
  sensor in the same frame gets the same value. Angles are averaged relative to the previous
  value, otherwise readings of 359 and 1 degrees would average to 180.

- Physics engine: every frame we predict where the ship should be from the commands we
  actually sent, then overwrite the prediction with every sensor that still works. Working
  sensors reduce the internal error, and the prediction fills in for broken sensors.

- Accounting for biases: we measured the thrusters and Rotate and correct for them both in
  the commands we send and in the physics prediction. Without these corrections, error would
  accumulate in our physics simulation, which led to early crashes.

3) Describe your strategy for dealing with thruster failures

   - Lander_Control and Safety_Override never touch Main/Left/Right_Thruster directly. They
   ask the agent for a thrust(ax, ay) and RobustAgent figures out how to fire what thrusters to
   fulfill this request.
   - If the main thruster and the side thruster we need both work, we stay upright and use
   them directly. So losing one side thruster only affects us when we want to push toward that
   side. If so, then we tilt the main thruster to get a sideways component instead, and we cap it
   so the tilt never yields more lift than what we requested.
   - Otherwise we point one working thruster along the thrust direction, whichever needs the least
   rotation, using THRUSTER_SWITCH_BIAS (45 degrees) to encourage the use of a same thruster so
   we don't flip-flop between two options. We only fire once we're within 30 degrees of the target
   angle, so we never push sideways or toward the ground while still turning. Tilt is limited
   to 60 degrees, which leaves spare lift on a side thruster for hovering.
   - Close above the platform (TOUCHDOWN_HEIGHT) we stay upright no matter what, since
   touchdown must be under 15 degrees.
   - If we lose the main thruster, being upright yields no lift. So we hover low on a side
   thruster, then at DROP_HEIGHT (36 px above the platform) go upright and do a
   free fall. We calculated that this would give an impact safely below the 10 m/s limit.
   - We also implemented boundaries on the map. If the craft drifts toward an edge, it
   automatically fires a thruster to push it back before it can fly off.

4) Describe your strategy for dealing with sensor malfunctions:

   For all of position, velocity and angle, we detect a failure the same way: a broken
   sensor returns random values, so the variance of our 12k samples shoots up. We
   measured healthy vs broken variance and put the limits in between. And we assume that
   once a sensor is broken, it stays broken, so we don't try for recovery of sensors.

   - Position sensors: the physics engine keeps predicting position from the (still
     working) velocity every frame, so a broken position sensor doesnt affect us too much.
   - Velocity sensors: velocity is predicted from the thrust we requested. Small errors
     would add up over time, so the gap between predicted and measured position is used
     for corrections.
   - Angle: Rotate never fails, so we track the angle by adding up our own Rotate commands.
   - Sonar: SONAR_DIST goes -1 everywhere when it dies, which is unfortunately the same as
     "nothing nearby", so we don't know the instant it breaks. So what we do is that
     we only mark it as broken when RangeDist sees something closer than 420 pixels while
     every beam keeps saying -1 for SONAR_BROKEN_FRAMES. Once broken, we build a fake
     sonar out of RangeDist, which never fails:
       * Every frame, the RangeDist reading is turned into a point on the map.
       * We remember the last 512 of these points, and every frame work out all 36 beams
         from where we are *now* to those points. Since we store where the ground is rather
         than distances, the beams stay correct as we move.
       * Every 120 frames we sweep RangeDist across +-60 degrees around our direction of
         travel to see what's ahead, but only if there's at least 100 px of ground below us
         and we're not falling fast, since the ship can't thrust while turning for a sweep.
     Safety_Override then uses this fake sonar just like it would the real one.

   What if you have multiple failures? what could you do if, for
   example both position and velocity sensors became inoperative?

   - When position sensors fail, we rely on their respective velocity sensors and vice
   versa. When sonar fails, we rely on RangeDist. When BOTH sensors on an axis fail, there's
   nothing left to measure that axis, so the physics engine predicts it purely from the
   commands we sent. That works as long as we know exactly what thrust was applied.
   - For Y specifically, there is some absolute truth left. RangeDist gives our height above
   the ground directly below while upright, and over the platform the ground height is just
   PLAT_Y. That could be used to correct the Y prediction during the final descent, which, at
   that point, Y correctness matters vastly more than x correctness

5) What was the hardest failure type to deal with (i.e. which is
   the component, either sensor or control that makes the lander
   harder to handle?)?

   - The hardest failure type to deal with was definitely Sonar. The main backbone of
   Safety_Override is Sonar, so without a fallback, it's completely blind. We had to rebuild
   it out of RangeDist, which only looks in one direction at a time, so we have to remember
   what it saw and rotate the ship to see more.
   - Second place was losing the main thruster on hard.ppm. The only way to land is a free
   fall, and with one thruster every sideways move also needs a rotation, which was very easy to
   get wrong near the terrain. Our early versions kept switching between the two side
   thrusters and never finished turning, which caused it to crash alot.

6) Assume a human pilot was in charge of controlling the craft
   (i.e. the pilot has control of thrusters and rotation).
   How would you change Safety_Override() so that it prevents
   the pilot from accidentally crashing the lander?

   - Safety_Override runs last, so whatever it commands is what physically happens
   regardless of what Lander_Control or a pilot asked for moments earlier. So we let the
   pilot fly freely while sensors are healthy. We implement additional checks
   to stop the pilot from crashing.
   - The problem is that we can't see what the pilot commanded, so our physics engine can't
   predict their effect. With healthy sensors that's not a problem, as the sensors correct it
   every frame. But the moment both sensors on one axis go blind, an untracked pilot input
   could ruin our predictions.
   So in that case we would take over only the controls whose effect we can no longer see,
   and re-issue our own commands for them every frame. the side thrusters if both X sensors
   are gone. the main thruster if both Y sensors are gone. rotation if the angle sensor
   is gone. But the pilot can keep other functionality that we deem safe for them to handle.

7) Do you think it is reasonable to write such a control function?
   (i.e. a routine that takes control from a human for their
    own safety)

    On one hand, I want to say yes, because humans make mistakes. Like the vast majority of
    transport accidents like Plane or Car crashes are due to human error. However, I also do
    not believe that code should be the sole captain. I personally believe that a human should
    be able to override any control in normal situations, because the code can also be wrong,
    and an alert pilot can manage that. Where I think it is reasonable is when a crash is clearly
    and undeniably imminent. In that case, a control function can step in only for as long as the
    danger lasts, and ideally with a way for the pilot to turn it off. That's also how we designed
    our Safety_Override. Outside of that, software should aide, give readings, suggestions, or manage
    some small components, but ultimately the pilot should have the veto power.

8) Should the pilot be aware that this software is present?
   (yes or no, and tell me why you think so!)

   - Yes. An override that shows up unannounced makes it look like the ship malfunctioning, rather
   than what it's doing, which is saving the people on the craft. If an unknowing,
   surprised pilot fights their own safety net, the results could be fatal, which defeats the purpose.

9) Would you buy/drive a car that you know has control software
   that will take control from you if it estimates you are
   doing something dangerous?

   - Yes, if it follows our philosophy. minimal, last-resort, with multiple fall-backs, and limited to clearly
   dangerous situations, such as the high-speed, incoming ground-approach checks we have in our code.
   It should not be a system that overrides judgment in ambiguous cases. So long that it minimizes damage and
   prioritizes lives, we have no problem using a system like that.

10) Can you as an embedded systems designer think of every
    possible scenario that could go wrong so as to write
    control software that ALWAYS works?

    Think about that...

    - No. Most of our worst bugs were things we never thought of until a test run crashed.
    For example, ships that spawn low and upside down above a hill, or a sonar sweep that
    turned off all thrust right above the ground. Not to mention, the two side thrusters
    "fighting" over which one should be used was particularly strange. None of these appeared
    from thinking through scenarios beforehand. We couldn't have thought of these. They came
    from running the simulation tens or even hundreds of times and reading logs of what went wrong.
    - A realistic goal shouldn't be to create software that always works, but to create software that
    fails gracefully, with pizzazz. It should keep working when one part breaks and have fallbacks for
    its fallbacks. Testing is also absolutely critical. Code should be tested as much as possible.
    Real life shouldn't be treated as a playground. Even then, the real world will unfortunately and
    eventually throw something at it that nobody tested.
